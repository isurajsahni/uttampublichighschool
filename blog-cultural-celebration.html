Cultural Celebration Brings Students Together

Published 15 December 2025

The school ground at Uttam Public High School looked completely different that morning. Marigold strings ran along the stage railing, folding chairs were lined up row after row, and a hum of excitement moved through every corridor as students in bright costumes waited for their turn. Our annual cultural celebration had arrived, and with it came a full day of music, dance, poetry and colour that brought the whole school family together.

What makes the day special is not only the performances themselves, but the fact that they belong entirely to the students. From choosing the songs to stitching the last sequins onto a dupatta, the celebration is built by the children of this school, with teachers guiding quietly from the wings.

A Stage Full of Colour and Rhythm

The celebration opened, as it always does in Punjab, with the dhol. A group of senior boys took the stage for bhangra, and within seconds the audience was clapping along to the beat. The energy carried straight into a giddha performance by the girls, whose boliyan drew laughter and loud cheers from the parents seated in the front rows. Watching students who are usually quiet in the classroom command the stage with such confidence is one of the joys of the day.

The programme that followed moved through many moods:

  • Group songs by the middle classes, including a devotional piece and a patriotic medley sung in three-part harmony
  • A skit on respecting elders, in which a young character learns to listen to his grandparents and rediscovers the value of family and community
  • Poetry recitation in Punjabi, Hindi and English, with students reciting both classic verse and lines they had written themselves
  • A traditional dress showcase featuring attire from Punjab and several other states, each participant explaining the meaning behind what they wore
  • A closing folk dance in which students from different grades performed together on one stage

The traditional dress showcase drew particular attention. Phulkari dupattas, juttis, turbans tied by fathers early that morning, and outfits borrowed from grandmothers turned the stage into a living display of heritage. Several students spoke about where their clothes came from and who in the family had worn them before, and those small stories held the audience completely.

Weeks of Rehearsal Behind Every Minute

None of it happened overnight. For close to a month, students stayed back after school to practise. Classrooms became rehearsal rooms, the corridor near the staff room turned into a costume store, and the ground echoed with dhol practice most evenings. Senior students helped juniors learn their steps, students who could sing coached those who were nervous, and children who had never spoken in front of a crowd slowly found their voice.

That rehearsal period does something no timetable can. Students learn to arrive on time because twenty other people are waiting. They learn to accept correction without taking it personally. They learn that a performance only works when everyone holds their place. By the time the curtain went up, the group had become a team.

What Days Like This Teach

At Uttam Public High School we treat cultural activity as part of education, not a break from it. A celebration of this kind builds:

  • Confidence on stage, and the ability to face an audience without fear
  • Discipline, through regular rehearsal, punctuality and preparation
  • Appreciation of heritage, in language, music, dress and everyday values
  • Cooperation across grades, as older and younger students work side by side
  • Creative thinking, in writing scripts, choosing music and designing costumes

These are the same qualities that serve a student in a classroom, in an examination hall and, later, in a workplace. A child who can hold a stage can also hold a viva. A child who has learned to rehearse patiently can also revise patiently.

Parents as Part of the Celebration

Parents filled the seats from the first item to the last. Many had helped at home with costumes, lines and props, and it showed in how closely they watched. Between performances, several came forward to thank the teachers for the effort put into preparing the children, and to say how much they had enjoyed seeing familiar folk traditions performed by their own sons and daughters. The applause after the skit on respecting elders was the loudest of the afternoon, which told us the message had landed exactly where it was meant to.

Since 2004, this school has held to a simple belief: culture and academics grow together. Strong results come from disciplined, confident and grounded students, and days like this one help build exactly that. We are proud of every child who danced, sang, recited or stood quietly backstage handing over a prop at the right moment, and we look forward to doing it all again next year.
